# [Dev Log] Precision Alerting and the Art of Content Curation

As a CTO, I’ve learned that the "last mile" of engineering isn't just about making a feature work—it's about how that feature communicates its state to the humans operating it. Today's work was a blend of refining critical infrastructure monitoring for our blockchain stack and managing a high-throughput content pipeline.

## 1. Refining the Signal: Separating Mainnet from Testnet

In any production environment, **alert fatigue** is a real danger. When a developer sees a notification in a "Mainnet Issue" channel, the adrenaline spike is immediate. However, if that channel is cluttered with Testnet errors, the psychological weight of the alert diminishes, and critical issues can be overlooked.

I discovered that our delegator watchers (`sigcheck` and `soak`) were bundling alerts for `del1`, `del2`, and `tn` (testnet) into a single message sent to the Mainnet room. This led to a situation where a Testnet error (increasing from 30 to 32) triggered a Mainnet alert.

### Technical Decision: Logical Routing in Shell
Instead of splitting these into entirely separate scripts—which would violate the DRY (Don't Repeat Yourself) principle and complicate state management—I implemented a "context-switching" logic within the loop.

```bash
# Inside the node loop
if [ "$NAME" = tn ]; then
  A0="$ALERT"; S0="$SUMMARY"; ALERT="$ALERT_TN"; SUMMARY="$SUMMARY_TN"; 
fi

# ... processing logic ...

if [ "$NAME" = tn ]; then
  ALERT_TN="$ALERT"; SUMMARY_TN="$SUMMARY"; ALERT="$A0"; SUMMARY="$S0"; 
fi
```

By temporarily swapping the alert buffers when the loop hits the `tn` node, I could collect Testnet-specific issues into a separate variable (`ALERT_TN`) and route them to a dedicated Testnet channel (`-1004406469969`), while keeping the promotion logic in the Mainnet room.

**Insight:** When building monitoring scripts, always assume the "Dry Run" will be used. I updated the `notify` function to explicitly print the destination chat ID during dry runs. This prevents the "9/11 incident" where fake alarms hit production channels because the developer didn't realize which target was active.

## 2. Content Engineering: Managing the "Text Problem" in AI Art

Outside of the blockchain stack, I've been scaling a book summary project using Flux (AI Image Generation). Today's focus was "The Shop That Sells Time" (시간을 파는 상점).

The technical challenge here is **visual noise**. In AI generation, "Modern Korea" often translates to "Signboard Hell"—images cluttered with illegible, distorted Korean text on shops, uniforms, and posters.

### The "Curation by Exclusion" Strategy
Rather than fighting the AI with complex negative prompts, I shifted to a **compositional strategy**. I analyzed the prompts and discarded 29 out of 80 images specifically because of "text pollution."

*   **Failed:** Bakery storefronts, noodle shops, school name tags.
*   **Successful:** Empty classrooms, hallways, rooftops, and close-ups of nature.

By intentionally avoiding "text-heavy" environments in the prompt design, I ensured a cleaner, more professional aesthetic for the final render.

## Closing Thoughts

Whether it's routing Telegram alerts for CPLABS's blockchain infrastructure or filtering AI-generated images for a content project, the core engineering principle is the same: **Noise Reduction**. 

In the first case, it's about reducing cognitive noise for the SRE team. In the second, it's about reducing visual noise for the viewer. Precision in what you *exclude* is often more important than what you *include*.
