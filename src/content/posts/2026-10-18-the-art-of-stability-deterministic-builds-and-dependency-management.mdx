# Engineering Log: Stability, Snapshots, and the Art of Dependency Management

As a CTO and engineer, I’ve spent over two decades navigating the evolution of software architecture. One thing I've learned is that while "building new features" gets the glory, "maintaining stability" is where the real engineering happens. Today's work was a reflection of that philosophy—focusing on infrastructure snapshots, dependency pinning, and operational auditing.

### The Core Focus: Ensuring Deterministic Builds

Looking at today's updates to my dashboard data, you'll see a series of commits across several critical repositories. While it looks like a simple data update on the surface, the underlying changes tell a story of risk mitigation.

One specific change caught my eye in the `key-backup-server` repository:
`build: pin test dependency org.web3j:crypto to 4.8.7`

In the world of Web3 and blockchain development, dependency drift is a silent killer. When you use dynamic versioning (like `latest` or range-based versions), a minor update in a third-party library can introduce subtle bugs in cryptographic signing or wallet derivation. By pinning `web3j:crypto` to a specific version (4.8.7), we ensure that our test environment is deterministic. If a build fails tomorrow, I know it’s because of a code change I made, not because a library author pushed a breaking change to a transitive dependency.

### The Strategy of Production Snapshots

I also spent a significant portion of the day managing production snapshots for several services, including the `mykeepin-identity-attestator`, `meta-auth-service`, and `mykeepin-sample-web-sp`.

You'll notice a pattern in the branch names: `prod-snapshot/20261017`. 

In high-stakes environments—especially those involving DID (Decentralized Identity) and authentication—relying solely on a `main` or `master` branch for production is a risk. I implement a "Snapshot Strategy" where the exact state of the code deployed to production is branched and tagged. This allows for:
1. **Instant Rollbacks:** If a deployment fails, we can revert to the exact snapshot of the previous day without hunting through commit histories.
2. **Auditability:** For compliance and security audits, we have a clear record of exactly what code was running at any given timestamp.

### Operational Insights: Infrastructure Right-Sizing

Beyond the code, I've been reviewing the `validator` repository, specifically regarding EBS IOPS and instance storage transitions. 

The commit message `"다운사이즈 폐기·인스턴스 스토리지 전환 검토안"` (Review of downsizing disposal and instance storage transition) highlights a constant struggle in blockchain node operation: balancing performance with cost. Moving from general-purpose EBS volumes to instance store (NVMe) can drastically reduce I/O wait times for blockchain databases, but it introduces the challenge of ephemeral storage. 

The technical decision here isn't just about "speed," but about designing a recovery mechanism that can handle the volatility of instance storage while maintaining the high throughput required for a validator.

### Final Thoughts

Today wasn't about writing a thousand lines of new logic; it was about tightening the screws. Whether it's through rigorous dependency pinning at **CPLABS** or meticulous production snapshotting, the goal is always the same: **reducing entropy**.

In a field as volatile as Web3 and AI, the most valuable engineer isn't the one who can code the fastest, but the one who can ensure the system stays up when the unexpected happens.
