# [Dev Log] Deep Dive into Blockchain Sync: Resolving "Invalid" Warnings and the Art of the Clean Sync

As a CTO and engineer, I’ve spent over two decades navigating the intersection of distributed systems and infrastructure. One thing I've learned is that in the world of blockchain, "success" isn't just about reaching the head of the chain—it's about the **integrity of the journey**.

Today's work focused on the final validation of a genesis clean sync for testnet 150 and the rigorous analysis of anomalies encountered during the process.

## The Challenge: The "Invalid" Warning Mystery

While the sync for testnet 150 reached the head successfully (taking approximately 57 hours and 32 minutes), a scan of the `start.log` (a massive 35GB file) revealed 6 instances of a specific warning:
`WARN Dropping peer for invalid blob sidecars … err="blob sidecar count mismatch"`

In a "clean sync" verification, any "invalid" string is a red flag. My primary goal was to determine if these were critical data integrity failures or benign network noise.

### Technical Analysis & Resolution

After diving into the code—specifically `eth/metadium_blobsync.go`—I traced the logic. The issue occurs when the node requests blob sidecars from a peer. If the number of sidecars returned doesn't match the number of blob transactions (`len(sidecars) != len(blobTxs)`), the node triggers a `count mismatch` and drops the peer.

**The insight:** I discovered that these specific blocks were from a period where sidecar preservation wasn't active across the network. The peers weren't sending "wrong" data; they were sending "empty" responses because the data simply didn't exist globally. 

This is a crucial distinction. The node interpreted a "missing" response as an "invalid" response, leading to the peer drop. I verified this by comparing the block hashes with the public dev RPC, confirming that the local data was correct and the "invalid" warning was a side effect of the fetch mechanism rather than a consensus failure.

## Engineering Decisions & Observations

1.  **The R4 Judgment:** Based on the analysis, I concluded that the sync was successful. I documented the "invalid" warnings as "explained" and submitted the R4 judgment to **CPLABS**, ensuring that the technical nuance (blob sidecar fetch vs. block validation) was clearly communicated.
2.  **Code Observation:** I noticed an inconsistency in the implementation. The `sync` path drops peers on empty sidecar responses, while the `PBFT` path simply continues. This is a valuable observation that I've flagged for the development team to ensure consistent behavior across different synchronization modes.
3.  **The Tmux Pitfall:** On a more practical note, I encountered a frustrating "ghost" issue where `send-keys` in a tmux window didn't trigger the command execution despite an Enter key being sent. I found that for long Korean strings, the standard Enter sometimes fails, and `C-m` (Carriage Return) is required to actually submit the input. A small but vital tip for anyone managing remote clusters via tmux.

## Final Thoughts

Whether it's a 35GB log file or a subtle bug in a Go slice length check, the devil is always in the details. A "successful" sync is only truly successful when you can explain every single warning in the logs.

Today's work reinforced the importance of cross-referencing code logic with real-time network behavior. By isolating the blob sidecar issue, we can move forward with the m1.2.0 candidate with full confidence.
