# The Art of Visibility: Automating the Engineering Pulse

As a CTO and engineer with over two decades in the trenches—spanning the evolution of early distributed systems to the current frontiers of Web3, DID, and AI—I’ve learned that the most dangerous state for a technical organization is **opacity**.

When you are managing complex architectures or leading teams at a scale like we do at **CPLABS**, the gap between "what is being worked on" and "what is actually being shipped" can widen quickly. This is why I am a firm believer in the "Engineering Pulse"—a way to visualize velocity and activity without the friction of manual reporting.

Today's work was a small but symbolic update to my personal dashboard data, but it reflects a larger philosophy on developer experience (DX) and observability.

### The Technical Implementation: Data-Driven Reflection

The change today was a straightforward update to `dashboard.json`:

```json
-  "generated_at": "2026-07-25T23:00:01.665Z",
+  "generated_at": "2026-07-26T23:00:01.485Z",
...
-          "last_msg": "Auto-generate weekly book post",
+          "last_msg": "Auto-generate weekly recap",
```

At first glance, this looks like a simple chore. However, this JSON file serves as the state layer for a custom-built activity tracker. Instead of relying on a third-party SaaS tool that abstracts away the raw data, I prefer a system that aggregates commit frequency, branch activity, and the nature of the messages across multiple repositories (from `jsong1230.github.io` to `metadium_homepage`).

### Why This Matters: The "Commit Velocity" Insight

Looking at the diff, you can see the `activity` arrays shifting. For instance, in the `jsong1230.github.io` repo, the commit count for the last 30 days ticked up to 71. 

In a professional setting—particularly when managing high-stakes blockchain infrastructure at **CPLABS**—tracking these patterns isn't about "micromanagement." It's about **pattern recognition**. 

When I see a sudden spike in activity on a `dev` branch followed by a silence on `main`, it tells me the team is in a heavy feature-build phase. Conversely, a steady stream of small commits across multiple repos usually indicates a stabilization or "polishing" phase. By automating the generation of this data, I remove the "reporting tax" from the engineer while maintaining a high-level view of the project's health.

### Personal Insight: Automation as a Mental Tool

I’ve spent years building AI-driven tools and DID frameworks, but I still find immense value in these simple automation scripts. The shift from "Auto-generate weekly book post" to "Auto-generate weekly recap" in the commit logs highlights a transition in how I'm processing my own technical growth.

The goal of any CTO should be to reduce the cognitive load on their team. Whether it's optimizing a CI/CD pipeline or creating a dashboard that visualizes work, the objective is the same: **Make the invisible visible.**

When the data is automated, the truth is objective. It allows us to stop asking "What did you do today?" and start asking "I see the velocity shifted here; do we have a blocker we need to solve?"

### Final Thoughts

Today was a reminder that the most effective tools are often the ones that run silently in the background. By maintaining a disciplined approach to tracking my own activity, I keep myself accountable and ensure that my technical trajectory aligns with my goals.

Stay curious, keep committing, and always automate the mundane.
