# Engineering the Feedback Loop: The Value of an Automated Development Dashboard

As a CTO and engineer with over two decades of experience, I’ve learned that the most dangerous state for a technical leader is "blindness"—not knowing exactly where the momentum is shifting across various projects until a deadline is missed or a bug surfaces. Whether I am architecting a DID system or scaling AI agents, visibility is the prerequisite for optimization.

Today's work was a "chore" in the git sense—updating the `dashboard.json` for my personal site—but it represents a larger technical philosophy I've applied throughout my career, including my time at **CPLABS**.

### The Problem: The Fragmentation of Focus

When you manage multiple repositories—ranging from validator nodes and AI agents (`claude-agent`) to content platforms (`heritageradio`)—it is easy to lose track of the "velocity" of each project. Standard git logs tell you *what* happened, but they don't easily tell you *where the energy is flowing* across a diverse portfolio of work.

To solve this, I maintain a data-driven dashboard that aggregates commit activity, last-modified dates, and commit counts over 7-day and 30-day windows.

### Technical Decisions: Data-Driven Visibility

The recent update to `src/data/dashboard.json` reflects a shift in my current development priorities. Looking at the diff, we can see a significant uptick in activity for specific modules:

```json
"name": "heritageradio",
"last_date": "2026-10-02",
"last_msg": "[admin] fix(admin): 5월 조회 범위를 1일부터...",
"commits_7d": 32,
"commits_30d": 152
```

By quantifying activity (the `activity` array and `commits_7d` metrics), I can objectively analyze my productivity. For instance, seeing `heritageradio` jump to 32 commits in a week while the `validator` repo remains stable tells me that my focus has shifted from infrastructure maintenance to feature refinement and administrative fixes.

### Insights from the Code

One interesting part of today's update is the `validator` repository's last message: 
`"bifrost validator 운영 런북 — 규칙·감시·장애대응·키 절차..."`

This highlights a critical transition in the lifecycle of any high-stakes system (like a blockchain validator): moving from **active development** to **operational stability**. Writing a "Runbook" (런북) is often less glamorous than writing new features, but from my experience at **CPLABS**, the difference between a professional system and an amateur one is the quality of its disaster recovery and operational documentation. 

If a validator kicks out a node at 3 AM, you don't want to be "innovating"—you want a checklist.

### Final Thoughts

While updating a JSON file might seem trivial, it is part of a larger system of self-monitoring. In the world of Web3 and AI, where the pace of change is exponential, the ability to zoom out and see your trajectory is invaluable. 

The goal isn't just to write more code, but to ensure that the code being written aligns with the strategic goals of the moment. Today's data shows a healthy balance between maintaining critical infrastructure (Validator) and iterating on user-facing products (Heritage Radio).

**Stay disciplined, stay visible.**
