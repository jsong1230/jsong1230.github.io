# Efficiency in Infrastructure: ALB Consolidation and the Perils of `unwrap()`

As a CTO, I often find that the most impactful work doesn't always come from writing new features, but from refining the "plumbing" of the system. Today was a prime example of this. My focus was split between high-level infrastructure optimization and the gritty reality of debugging production crashes in a cross-chain environment.

## Infrastructure Optimization: The ALB Consolidation

One of the primary goals at **CPLABS** has been reducing operational overhead and cloud expenditure without sacrificing stability. Today, I completed the third phase of our Application Load Balancer (ALB) consolidation, migrating the mainnet explorer (Web and WebSocket) onto the API hub.

The technical challenge here wasn't just moving traffic, but managing complex routing rules. In a large-scale environment, rule priority is everything. I encountered a potential "trap" where a pre-existing priority 100 rule was designed to route all `/api/v1/*` traffic to a specific service (mchat), which would have hijacked the explorer's traffic.

**The Decision:** Instead of modifying the existing complex rule set—which risks regression—I implemented a "priority override." By placing the front-end and WebSocket host rules at **p10 and p20**, I ensured they were evaluated first.

> *“front/ws rules placed at p10/p20 ahead of the any-host /api/v1/*”*

Interestingly, this process revealed a pre-existing misrouting bug for `bunjaws.metadium.club`. The DNS was already pointing to the API-LB, but the WebSocket rules only existed on the Web-LB. By consolidating, I didn't just save costs; I actually fixed a silent routing error.

**The Result:** Seven old ALBs are now pending deletion, saving approximately **$154/month**. While the dollar amount is modest, the reduction in architectural complexity is where the real value lies.

## The "Null Glitch" and the Danger of `unwrap()`

On the operational side, I spent time analyzing a crash loop in the Bifrost relayer. The watchdog triggered an alert after 83 panics within a 90-minute window.

The root cause was a classic combination of an external glitch and fragile code. The Bitcoin provider (dRPC) intermittently returned a `null` value for `getblockcount`. In the relayer's Rust code, the developer used `.unwrap()` on this result. In Rust, `unwrap()` is a "nuclear option"—if the value is `None` or an `Err`, the thread panics immediately.

**Insight from the Crash:**
This is the second distinct location in the vendor's code where an unchecked `unwrap()` has caused a production crash. It serves as a stark reminder: **Never trust external API responses.** Whether it's a blockchain RPC or a REST API, every single call must be handled with graceful error recovery (e.g., using `match` or `if let`) rather than assuming the provider will always adhere to the spec.

I've documented this as a "second panic point" to be reported to the vendor. For now, the system self-recovered, and I've pre-verified a swap candidate (`publicnode`) to ensure we can pivot quickly if the glitch returns.

## Final Thoughts

Today’s work highlights the duality of the CTO role: one moment you are calculating cost-efficiencies and optimizing network topology, and the next, you are diving into the depths of a Rust panic log to identify a vendor bug. 

Maintaining a lean infrastructure while ensuring the system is resilient against "null glitches" is a constant balancing act. But that is exactly where the engineering discipline of **CPLABS** shines—turning incidents into documentation and waste into efficiency.
